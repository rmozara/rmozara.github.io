World Amoeba

English · German

Sometimes, when we meditate, we can distance ourselves a little more than usual from the phenomenal world, and our perception expands beyond the domain that we are immediately experiencing through our physical senses. We become aware of the sounds around us as they enter our field of consciousness. The sounds take shape within us, and we project them outward again, so that we experience them as existing outside ourselves. We become aware of the surface beneath us. It too first appears within us and is then projected outward from there. We become aware of our breathing and our body and feel them as part of our active being. Yet they are only one part of it. At the same time, we become aware of that which perceives: something that exists quietly and silently in itself. It is connected with the perceptions mediated through the body and with the perception of the breath, and yet these seem both connected with this silent consciousness and separate from it — as in a surreal image. All these perceptions take place within this field of consciousness and are at the same time projected outward from it. As we perceive them, this field extends as far as the things it encompasses, beyond the boundaries of our body.

When we are balanced, perhaps even a little tired, so that some of the limiting functions of the mind begin to lose their force, further phenomena can gradually appear that extend beyond immediate physical perception, including the perception of our own body. These perceptions are not as clear as direct sensory impressions and do not yet extend far beyond the immediate field of experience, but they feel just as real. We become aware of a consciousness that has no form, that seems to stand opposite the phenomenal world and yet remains connected with it. It is calm and static, but dynamic at its edges. It appears larger than the phenomenal world. Only where it enters into appearances is it bounded by them. It is colorless and empty and at the same time filled with stillness. It does not change; it simply exists out of itself.

And sometimes it seems as though this consciousness intervenes in the things of the world. Then it appears to move them, as though it were extending invisible arms — arms that are not really arms. Just as an amoeba moves the small particles around it, so it moves appearances: it extends itself, surrounds something, lifts it to another place, and withdraws again. The movement seems almost mechanical and yet fluid, gentle, and without friction. It expresses itself in the bird when it chirps; in the horn when a person sounds it; in the movements of people when they move something from one place to another, whether themselves or an object. It expresses itself by inhabiting the forms in which this phenomenal world takes shape. It acts on different levels: in the movements of animals, in plants moving in the wind, in inanimate matter, for instance in the wind itself, and likewise in human thought.

Even when we sit in the world with our eyes open, we can see the movements of this consciousness. It appears like a foam through which larger movements propagate, at times coherently, like waves, with rhythms that form and dissolve again. Individual human beings appear within it like bubbles, each one an individuation of its own. Light shimmers across their surfaces in rainbow colors. Thus they sit beside one another in a room, together with their thoughts and other mental formations, and interact with one another. Each is occupied with themselves and identified with their thoughts to varying degrees, while at the same time communicating with the others. They do not notice that something greater is communicating with itself within all this. The one quiet, silent consciousness has entered into this phenomenal world, appears here as multiplicity, and talks with itself.

Its individuations are separate from one another and yet part of the same movement. And the deeper one looks into this multiplicity, the further individuation continues. On every level considered in itself, the movements appear calm, fluid, and almost mechanical. Seen from progressively larger scales, however, the movements of the levels below appear increasingly fine and rapid, until they begin to flicker like innumerable fine cilia. Below the level of the individual, too, this structure continues across ever smaller scales: through organs, tissues, and cells down to molecules and atoms, whose movements appear increasingly fine and flickering when viewed from the levels above them. No continuity seems possible within this realm of separations, and yet the space is completely filled by them.

Everywhere within this foam, pairs of eyes look around. They look at one another and see one another, but they do not see the whole. Even the whole does not recognize itself as a whole in these gazes. This consciousness has unfolded here into a world of multiplicity in which its individuations look at one another, speak with one another, react to one another, and move the things around them. A vast, sticky world of eyes, mouths, and arms: eyes watching one another, mouths speaking with one another, arms gesturing, grasping, and shifting things from place to place. Everything belongs to the same movement. Just as the protrusions of an amoeba extend outward from its body, surround something, and withdraw again, so this consciousness, multiplied into countless individuations, moves through the world and at the same time moves the things within it.

If we follow this multiplicity in the opposite direction and turn our attention toward ever larger contexts, the separate individuations combine into ever larger social formations, which in turn take on organismic traits. Groups, institutions, states, and federations of states form new units with boundaries, organs of perception and communication, memories, and capacities for action of their own. What says “I” at the level of the individual human being seems to return on these larger scales in increasingly complex forms.

Large states appear as immensely complex amoebas with millions or billions of arms, eyes, and mouths. Their bodies consist of buildings, roads, and cities; their organs take shape as administrations, research institutions, legal systems, social structures, and economic and military complexes. Through all these organs they perceive their surroundings, collect and store information, plan, communicate, act, and react to one another. Their borders act like membranes without completely sealing these formations off from one another; perception and influence extend far beyond those borders. Thus, even formations separated by great distances can probe one another, constrain each other's ranges of movement, evade one another, or press against one another.

At the same time, they remain in a continuous exchange of matter with their surroundings and with other such formations: raw materials, energy, goods, people, and information flow through their membranes, are taken in, processed, passed on, or released again. When two such formations come into conflict, an immense number of individual movements seems to contract into a single multilayered movement. Millions of people, institutions, and technological systems interlock like the limbs and organs of a complex organism. Stored knowledge forms its memory, and through the techniques it has developed, this organism intervenes in its environment. The immense formations apprehend and observe one another, react, reach toward each other, or withdraw, almost as though one were watching two extraordinarily complex amoebas struggle with one another under a microscope. And here too, the one quiet, silent consciousness appears divided within the world of multiplicity and has not yet recognized itself within it.
